Question 1

What does being late actually cost?

A question of consequence. It prices a day of lateness, and that price is what sizes every investment argued for later.

Question 1 · consequence

A late day costs two stars. An early day earns nothing.

Average review score by days early or late. Switch corridor to see the same shape everywhere

The line is flat to the left of the expected date. Arriving four days early scores no better than arriving on the day.
It falls off a cliff the moment the date passes, then flattens again.
So very late is barely worse than slightly late. The damage is done at day one.

One day of lateness is worth about two stars. That is the exchange rate for every decision in this deck.

Question 1 · consequence

One-star reviews are a delivery problem, not a product problem

Where one-star reviews come from. Move the threshold to test how sensitive the split is

Delivery failures account for the majority of the worst reviews at every definition of “bad”. Stars are what the marketplaces rank on, so this is the currency the rest of the deck is priced in.

Question 2

How does the gap between expected and actual delivery behave, across orders and over time?

A question of magnitude. It decides how much slack the expected date needs, and how aggressive a date the customer can safely be shown.

Question 2 · magnitude

The expected date is padded, and most orders land far inside it

Every order by how many days early or late it arrived against its own expected date

Customers are told about 23.2 days and receive the parcel in about 10.2. 79.0% arrive a week or more early. The 6.8% that are late are what survives that buffer, and the buffer has been shrinking.

Question 2 · magnitude

Two lines, and the space between them

The expected date against the 9-in-10 delivery line, by purchase month

Violet is the date customers are shown; blue is what the network delivers to 9 in 10 orders. While violet sits above blue, only about a tenth of orders can miss.
March 2018: blue climbs through violet. The network slowed and overran the buffer from below.
August 2018: the lines never cross. Violet is cut down onto blue until only 0.4 of a day of buffer is left.

The gap closes twice, for opposite reasons. Naming which one is the whole decision.

Question 2 · the finding

The network got faster and lateness rose five-fold.

In June 2018 the slowest tenth took 15.9 days, customers were told 27.3, and 1.2% arrived late, the best month in the data. In August the network was faster still at 13.1 days, the quickest month of all, and 6.2% arrived late, because the expected date had been cut from 27.3 days to 13.4. A network problem costs carrier contracts and quarters. An expected-date problem costs a number in a system, this week. Olist entered the last month of the data with the second kind.

Question 3

Where in the journey does delivery time vary most, seller handling or carrier transit?

A question of ownership. It decides whose problem this is: seller enforcement, carrier contracts, or Olist taking control of fulfilment itself.

Question 4

How does reliability change with the distance goods travel, and does the expected date stretch with it?

A question of geography. It decides whether one national standard is achievable, or whether the standard has to be tiered by corridor.

Question 4 · geography

The expected date stretches with distance, but not far enough

Orders grouped by distance travelled. Switch the lens

On the median the buffer only widens, 8.7 to 14.2 days. Nothing looks wrong anywhere.
On the slowest tenth it shrinks from +2.4 to -1.9 days, going negative past 1,500 km: the date sits below the line the network reaches.
Share late follows it exactly, 4.4% to 11.7%.

Same orders, opposite conclusion. The lens decides the answer.

Question 4 · geography

What predicts lateness is the buffer, not the map

What to look at

  • On buffer it snaps into a line: -0.88.

Question 4 · geography

By August the problem region had changed, and no average would show it

Share of orders late by region: the whole period against August 2018 alone

Across the full window the Northeast is worst and São Paulo best. In August that inverts: São Paulo becomes the worst region in the country at 9.0% late, on a date cut to 9.4 days, while the Northeast still had 25.7 and delivered 2.1%.

Question 5

How does performance behave when order volume surges, and does every region absorb it equally?

A question of stress. It decides what peak capacity to plan for Black Friday, and where to pre-position it.

Question 5 · stress

Only one of the four shocks was a demand shock

Orders per week above; below, switch between what the customer saw, what the network did, and what Olist told them

Black Friday ran at 2.34× the autumn baseline. The other three carry no comparable rise, and the truckers' strike is a collapse. Capacity for a surge is not what protects the expected date.

Question 5 · stress

The same ten-point diagnostic, run on every shock

Every measurable characteristic of the orders in each episode, against the four weeks either side

Black Friday: orders per day +118%. The instrument works: it finds a real shock when there is one.
Feb–Mar 2018: orders per day +6%, every composition measure flat, and the carrier tail still grew 8.0 days.
Truckers' strike: orders per day −41%, and Olist added 4.5 days to the date.

Nothing inside Olist's own operation explains February. That is a specific question for the carrier, not a shrug.

Priced against the two-year average the standard is +4.5 days and reads as a tweak. Priced from where August actually left the business it is +12.6.

The decision

Three options, one recommendation

Option 1

Keep August's expected date

Leave it at 13.4 days, the shortest Olist has ever shown. Costs nothing to do nothing.

Against: São Paulo showed 9.4 days and was 9.0% late, the worst region in the country, while the network ran faster than at any point in two years.

Option 2 · recommended

A 95% standard, tiered by corridor

20 days for São Paulo, 30 for the South, 32 for the rest of the Southeast, 29 for the Centre-West, 41 for the Northeast, 44 for the North.

For: +12.6 days, no capital, reversible in a week, and the only lever shown to move the late rate. Against: a longer date may cost orders, and this data cannot measure that.

Option 3

Build the network

Own warehouses and cross-docking, the way Mercado Livre did between 2017 and 2018.

Against, for now: US$425m of shipping subsidies in 2018 and seventeen points of gross margin, built in São Paulo, the corridor where Olist is already fastest. It is the 2020 answer.

Why 95: it is not borrowed from elsewhere. It is what São Paulo already delivers, at 95.5%. 90% writes one late order in ten into the standard itself; 98% pays for variance Olist does not control.

Commit to 95%, set the date per corridor, and start by reversing the August cut in São Paulo.

If it worked

On-time holds near 95% after Black Friday 2018 and orders do not fall. The expected date was the problem: make the standard permanent.

If it did not

On-time still misses in the Northeast and Rio at these lengths. The constraint is carrier capacity, the date has gone as far as it usefully can, and Option 3 comes back.

If it cost us

Orders fall materially. The cost this analysis could not measure is real: re-cut at 90%, which the table prices at +7.5 days rather than +12.6.

The test: read on-time rate and order count by corridor for the two weeks after Black Friday 2018, against the same two weeks of 2017.

One number, one week, one reversible decision, and a test that can prove it wrong.
